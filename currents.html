Vol. I · A Literary Gathering Room

Currents

A drawing-room of letters from the literary deep.

“A book must be the axe for the frozen sea within us.”

— Franz Kafka, letter to Oskar Pollak, 27 January 1904

Currents is the press’s gathering room — an ongoing series of short essays on the writers we keep returning to (Joyce, Woolf, Fitzgerald, O’Connor, Hemingway, and the long company of writers they made possible), on questions of craft, and on the books we find worth passing along. Newest entries appear first. Each is a small conversation, not a definitive judgment. Some conversations stay small. Others widen, over time, into the full scholarly studies our authors publish elsewhere — a current finding, eventually, a river.

What you will find here:

  • Reflections on the writers we return to — the classics, the moderns, the rooms they made.
  • Notes on the writing life — questions of craft, problems of form.
  • Editorial counsel — advice from the desk on revision, voice, and patience.
  • Recommended reading — books we believe deserve a careful audience.
  • Letters from readers — your own thoughts, selected and lightly edited.

Join the Currents

Readers are welcome here. If a passage has stayed with you, if you have a question about craft, a book you wish more readers knew, or a thought on one of the writers we love — write to us. Selected letters appear in Currents, attributed by first name and city, or anonymously if you prefer.

Send to [email protected] with ‘Currents’ in the subject line. Keep it under 400 words. We read everything and reply to what we publish.

Where the River Widens: Notes Toward a Full Study of All We Ever Needed

By The Editors · August 2026

Every so often a short dispatch here outgrows the room. That happened this month with James Mulhern's story collection All We Ever Needed — eight stories in which love and harm are so often administered by the same hand that the reader stops expecting them to arrive separately. What began as a note on a single line from the book's closing pages ("For a few moments, the three of us had all we ever needed") became, over several drafts, a full scholarly essay on the collection's themes, motifs, archetypes, allusion, and craft. We are publishing the complete study on the author's own site rather than compress it here.

The Word That Comes Back

By The Editors · August 2026

There is a kind of poem that turns on a single word — a word so ordinary it might have gone unnoticed the first time it was spoken. Marie Howe does this in "What the Living Do," where the word is lovely. Frank O'Hara does it in "The Day Lady Died," where the word is practically. What these poems know is that recurrence, in the domestic register, is how grief actually behaves.

Vol. I · No. IV · MMXXVI

The Sentence That Refuses to Name Itself

By The Editors · July 2026

There is a moment, near the end of any great short story, when the writer could name the thing and does not. The scene arrives at its climax. The reader understands. And then the sentence turns aside, refuses the noun, and gives you a curtain, a window, a train ticket, a shoe. Chekhov taught modern short fiction this move. Every writer who has written a serious short story since has been trying, one way or another, to earn it.

The Grandmother in the Doorway

By The Editors · June 2026

Some writers spend a career going somewhere. Others spend a career coming back to the same place. Faulkner did the second. Woolf did the second. Marilynne Robinson has done the second for forty years and won every prize a writer can win for the going and never-leaving. The recurring figure — the grandmother in the doorway, the lighthouse at the end of the water — is not a failure of imagination. It may be the most disciplined thing a writer can do.

Fitzgerald, at the Edge of a Sentence

By The Editors · April 2026

The ending of Babylon Revisited is one of the great quiet endings in American fiction. Fitzgerald, who rewrote the final paragraph more than once, refuses the consolations a lesser writer would have reached for. There is no flourish, no rounding image. The widowed Charlie Wales is left alone in a Paris bar at Christmas, and the prose declines to comfort him.

A Book Worth Knowing

By The Editors · February 2026

If you have not read William Maxwell's So Long, See You Tomorrow, set aside the hundred and thirty-five pages it will ask of you and give them gladly. It is the story of a murder on an Illinois farm in 1921 and of a boy who, fifty years later, is still trying to write down what he should have said to another boy at school.
